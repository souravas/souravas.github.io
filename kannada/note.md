# Overview

Kannada Gottilla means "I don't know Kannada" — the phrase the first course is named after. This note is every Kannada course Sourav has worked through, merged into one ladder of 89 lessons, one lesson a day, running from the sound system to full-speed Bangalore speech.

Three blocks. **Beginner** is Lesson 1 to Lesson 33: the sounds, the pronouns, the possessives, the numbers, the three tenses and the two ways to ask a question. **Intermediate** is Lesson 34 to Lesson 69: the grammar that turns words into sentences, the vocabulary lists, and the situations you actually stand in. **Advanced** is Lesson 70 to Lesson 89: the helper verbs, the emphasis endings and the listening habits that separate a finished course from a person who lives here.

Four sources feed it.

- **Kannada Gottilla** ran as a WhatsApp group, one lesson a day, each lesson pushed as a short audio recording with the words written out. 26 lessons and a final assessment. Two of its lessons were audio only, a revision walk-through and a set of study tips, so nothing of them survives here except the advice to play a recording on a walk or in traffic. Its formula sheet, handed out as a PDF and a spreadsheet, is Lesson 9 below. The audio recordings are not in the vault.
- **[Learn Kannada](https://www.udemy.com/course/learn-kannada/)** by Bhashafy Languages on Udemy. Four weeks, 37 lessons, transcribed from the course slides. It supplied the sound system, the kinship names, the full conjugation tables, the vocabulary lists, the postpositions, the five stories and the two long conversations.
- A YouTube playlist by [Masala Chai Media](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLaXrlhEl6rFL8LZ7Gf0UKlEJLTTNkUTk_), started and dropped. It supplied the near forms of the third party pronouns, the rest of the greetings, and the festival wishes.
- The lessons past what any of the three covered, added here so the day-a-lesson ladder keeps going past greetings and word lists.

The goal is narrow: speak Kannada, and understand Kannada spoken at full speed. Reading the script is not a goal, so there is no alphabet lesson below. Lesson 1 covers the sounds you have to hear and say, with the Kannada letter beside each one, and Lesson 68 covers the contractions that make a full-speed sentence sound like a wall.

Everything is spoken Bangalore Kannada — `tagOLi` rather than the written `tegedukoLLi` — with the written form named where it differs enough to matter.

A native speaker will phrase some of this another way. The conjugation, case, negation and sound tables were checked against reference grammars; the situational phrases in Lessons 58 to 67 were not, so those are the ones to run past a Kannada speaker first. Treat the tables as a working default, not a rule book.

# How This Note Writes Kannada

Every Kannada cell starts lowercase, even a full sentence, so a capital letter always marks a sound and never the start of a sentence. Names and English words keep their usual capitals.

A capital `L`, `D`, `T` or `N` inside a word is retroflex. A capital `E`, `I` or `O` is a long vowel, and so is a doubled `aa`, `ee` or `oo` — `I` and `ee` are the same sound. So `naaLe`, `koDi`, `enTu`, `hEge` and `iddIra` are not typos. Lesson 1 has the whole set. Names keep their usual English spelling, which hides two sounds you will say every day: Kannada is said `kannaDa`, with a retroflex `D`, and Bengaluru is said `bengaLooru`, with a retroflex `L` and a long `oo`.

A hyphen inside a Kannada word marks the join between a root and an ending — `maaD-taa iddEne`, `mane-ge`, `ell-inda`. It is a teaching aid and not part of the spelling. Kannada writes all of these as one word and you say them as one word. Where a vowel meets a vowel at the join, a `y` slides in to bridge it, so `mane-alli` is said and written `maneyalli`. After a final `a` the bridge is usually a `d`, and this note writes that one in — `baNNa-dalli`, said `baNNadalli` — because nothing in the sound predicts it.

Three pairs of endings mean the same thing, and you will meet both halves of each pair.

|Form|Also said as|Which one this note uses|
|---|---|---|
|`-utta`|`-taa`|both, and they are the same ending|
|`-uttEne`, `-uttEve`|`-tEne`, `-tEve`|the short one, except where a line names the written form|
|`-tEne`, `-tEve`|`-tIni`, `-tIvi`|`-tEne`, though Bangalore says `-tIni` more often|

That last pair is worth reading twice. `naanu bartEne` and `naanu bartIni` are both correct and both mean I will come. This note writes `-tEne` and `iddEne` so that one form runs all the way through. The Bhashafy course teaches `-tIni` and `iddIni` throughout, so expect to hear the second one far more than the first. Only the first person changes; every other ending in the language is the same either way.

# Daily Routine

About twenty minutes a day, in this order. The order matters more than the minutes: review before new, out loud before silent, and one real use before the day ends.

1. **Review.** Read yesterday's lesson out loud, then the lesson from a week ago. The second read is the one that makes it stick; a lesson met once is gone in a fortnight.
2. **Learn.** Read today's lesson with every Kannada line said out loud, never in your head. Then cover the Kannada column and say each line from the English. A table you can read but cannot say is not learned yet.
3. **Make it yours.** Say three sentences about your own life in today's pattern. On Lesson 18 that is what you are doing right now, not what Emily is doing.
4. **Listen.** Five minutes of real Kannada by the method in Lesson 88 — one short clip, the same clip all week. Start on day one, not day 88: you will catch almost nothing for weeks, and the catching is the slowest thing here to grow.
5. **Use one line.** Say one thing from the last few lessons to a real person — an auto driver, a shopkeeper, a colleague. One line a day is 89 conversations by the end.

Three rules keep it going. A heavy lesson — 12, 29, 37 — can take two days, because the number is a lesson, not a deadline. A missed day restarts at the review, not at the next lesson. And progress lives on the Kannada line of [[50 - Practice/01 - Now|Now]], as `— Lesson N` after the link, so move it there when a lesson is done.

Nothing here has audio, so borrow a voice. Once a week, ask a Kannada speaker to read that week's new lines while you record them on your phone, then play the recording on a walk or in traffic, the one habit the WhatsApp course left behind.

# Beginner

Lesson 1 to Lesson 33. The sounds, the words and the three tenses. At the end of this block you can introduce yourself, ask and answer a question, and say what you did yesterday and what you will do tomorrow.

## Lesson 1 — Sounds That Change the Meaning

You do not need the script. You do need the sounds, because Kannada separates pairs that English runs together, and a wrong one turns a word into a different word. Learn this lesson before the word lists start, or you will learn half the vocabulary wrong.

Retroflex against dental. For a retroflex sound curl the tongue back and touch the roof of the mouth well behind the teeth. For a dental sound put the tongue tip against the back of the top teeth. This note writes the retroflex one with a capital.

|Letter|This note writes|Sound|Example|
|---|---|---|---|
|ಟ|T|retroflex t|ooTa — meal|
|ತ|t|dental t|tande — father|
|ಡ|D|retroflex d|koDi — give|
|ದ|d|dental d|dina — day|
|ಣ|N|retroflex n|haNNu — fruit|
|ನ|n|dental n|nanna — my|
|ಳ|L|retroflex l|haaLu — ruined|
|ಲ|l|plain l|haalu — milk|

`L` and `l` are the pair that trips up a beginner most, because English has only one l. These are not fine distinctions. They are separate words.

|Pair|Meaning|
|---|---|
|haalu / haaLu|milk / ruined|
|halli / haLLi|lizard / village|
|bele / beLe / bELe|price / crop / lentil|
|kallu / kaLLa|stone / thief|
|Odu / ODu|read / run|

Eight more pairs arrive later and catch people the same way. Learn each one as a pair on the day it turns up.

|Pair|Meaning|Lesson|
|---|---|---|
|sEbu / seebe|apple / guava|50|
|kOLi / kOti|chicken / monkey|52, 53|
|aNNa / anna|elder brother / cooked rice|11, 52|
|haNNu / haNa|fruit / money|50, 72|
|kai / kaayi|hand / unripe fruit|54, 50|
|kaalu / kallu|leg / stone|54, 1|
|hattu / hattira|ten / near|12, 36|
|maga / magaLu|son / daughter|11|

Vowel length is the other half. A long vowel is held about twice as long, and this note writes it either doubled or as a capital.

|Letter|Short|Letter|Long|
|---|---|---|---|
|ಅ|a — mane, house|ಆ|aa — maatu, word|
|ಇ|i — idu, this|ಈ|ee — neeru, water; also written I, as in iddIra|
|ಉ|u — kuDi, drink|ಊ|oo — ooTa, meal|
|ಎ|e — keLage, below|ಏ|E — kELu, ask|
|ಒ|o — hoge, smoke|ಓ|O — hOgu, go|

Two more vowels come up often enough to name: `ai` as in `aidu`, five, and `au` as in `aushadhi`, medicine.

A doubled consonant is held long the same way. `kate` is a story and `katte` is a donkey. `hattu` is ten and `hatu` is nothing.

Three things you can stop worrying about. The aspirated consonants in `dhanyavaada`, `bhaashe` and `shubha` are barely aspirated in Bangalore speech, so under-doing them costs you nothing. The two sh sounds have merged for most speakers. And a light English accent on the rest is fine, as long as the retroflex sounds and the vowel lengths are right, because those are the two that carry meaning.

## Lesson 2 — Word Order and the Missing Verb

Three rules explain almost every short sentence in this note.

**The verb goes last.** English is subject-verb-object and Kannada is subject-object-verb, so *I do yoga* comes out as *I yoga do*.

|Language|Order|Example|
|---|---|---|
|English|subject - verb - object|I do yoga|
|Kannada|subject - object - verb|naanu yOga maaDtEne|

**There is no am, is or are.** A sentence that only links two things drops the verb.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I am Sourav.|naanu Sourav|
|this is our house.|idu namma mane|
|he is a student.|avanu vidyaarthi|
|that is the Mysore Palace.|adu Mysore Palace|

**The articles a, an and the are optional.** `obba` is a or an for a man, `obbaLu` for a woman and `ondu` for a thing, and all three are left out more often than not.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I am a student.|naanu vidyaarthi|
|I am a student.|naanu obba vidyaarthi|
|there is a pen there.|alli ondu pen ide|

Spoken and written Kannada differ mostly in the verb ending, and the two registers sit side by side all through this note.

|Register|Example|English|
|---|---|---|
|spoken|naanu yOga maaDtEne|I do yoga|
|written|naanu yOga maaDuttEne|I do yoga|

## Lesson 3 — Greetings

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|hello|namaskaara|
|good morning|shubhOdaya|
|good afternoon|shubha madhyaana|
|good evening|shubha sanje|
|good night|shubha raatri|
|good day|shubha dina|
|welcome, please come in|banni|
|welcome, formal|suswaagata|
|thank you|dhanyavaada|
|many thanks, more formal|dhanyavaadagaLu|
|wishes|shubhaashayagaLu|
|congratulations|abhinandanegaLu|
|well done|shabhaash|
|all the best|shubhavaagali|
|take care|hushaaru|
|no problem, it is alright|paravaagilla|
|how are you?|hEg-iddIra?|
|I am fine.|chennaag-iddEne|
|goodbye|hOgi bartEne|

`shabhaash` is well done to a person; `abhinandanegaLu` is congratulations on an occasion. `paravaagilla` is the standard answer to an apology, and it also means not bad. It contracts to `parvaagilla` in speech.

`dhanyavaadagaLu` is `dhanyavaada` plus the plural ending `-gaLu` from Lesson 7. The plural is the more formal of the two.

Three of these are literal and worth taking apart. `hEg-iddIra` is *how you-are*. `chennaag-iddEne` is *good I-am*. And `hOgi bartEne` is *I will go and come* — Kannada has no goodbye that only means goodbye, because announcing you are leaving for good is rude, so you say you will be back. `bartEne` on its own does the same job.

The `shubha` greetings are the written register, the kind that arrive on a card or a WhatsApp forward. Out loud, Bangalore says *good morning* and *good night* in English, and the greeting you will actually be given is a question about food.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|have you eaten? The everyday greeting.|ooTa aaytaa?|
|have you had breakfast?|tinDi aaytaa?|
|yes, I have. And you?|aaytu, nimdu?|

It is not an invitation and it is not really about food. Answer `aaytu` and ask it back, the way English answers *how are you* with *fine*. Lesson 26 takes the words apart.

Wishing someone on a festival is one formula: the name of the festival, then `habbada shubhaashayagaLu`. `habba` is festival.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|happy Diwali|deepaavaLi habbada shubhaashayagaLu|
|happy Christmas|Christmas habbada shubhaashayagaLu|
|happy Holi|hOLi habbada shubhaashayagaLu|
|happy birthday|huTTuhabbada shubhaashayagaLu|
|happy Ugadi, the Kannada new year|yugaadi habbada shubhaashayagaLu|
|happy new year|hosa varshada shubhaashayagaLu|
|happy Kannada Rajyotsava, 1 November|Kannada raajyOtsavada shubhaashayagaLu|

The new year and Kannada Rajyotsava, the day Karnataka was formed, are not a `habba`, so the ending goes straight onto their own names.

## Lesson 4 — Yes, No, Please and Sorry

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|yes|houdu|
|no|illa|
|no, it is not|alla|
|okay|sari|
|please|dayaviTTu|
|forgive me, sorry|kshamisi|
|what?|Enu?|
|how much?|eshTu?|
|let us go|hOgONa|

Kannada has two noes here and they are not interchangeable. `illa` denies existence or an action — there is not, I do not. `alla` denies an identity — it contradicts a false statement about what a thing is. There is a third, `bEDa`, and Lesson 34 puts all three side by side.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I want|bEku|
|I do not want|bEDa|
|I want water.|nanage neeru bEku|
|I do not want water.|nanage neeru bEDa|

The sentence starts with `nanage`, to me, and not with `naanu`. That is not a slip. Lesson 38 explains why, and it is the single most useful pattern in the language.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I do not know Kannada.|nanage Kannada gottilla|
|do you know English?|nimage English gottaa?|

## Lesson 6 — Pronouns, Far and Near

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I|naanu|
|we|naavu|
|you, one person, younger or informal|neenu|
|you, many people, older or formal|neevu|

Age decides which you to use. Speak to someone older with `neevu` even when you address one person only.

Every third person pronoun comes in a far form and a near form. The far form starts with `a` and means that; the near form starts with `i` and means this. Nothing else in the word changes.

|English|Far — a|Near — i|
|---|---|---|
|he, informal|avanu|ivanu|
|she, informal|avaLu|ivaLu|
|they, or he or she formally|avaru|ivaru|
|it, that or this|adu|idu|
|those or these, for things|avu|ivu|
|there, here|alli|illi|

`avaru` is both the plural they and the polite singular. Use it for one person you respect, the same way `neevu` works in the second person. A parent always takes it.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|he is my friend.|ivanu nanna friend|
|that is his bag.|adu avana bag|
|this is a charger.|idu charger|
|that is a wall.|adu gODe|
|she is Geeta.|avaLu Geeta|
|she is Sunita, formal|avaru Sunita|
|this is the Vidhana Soudha.|idu Vidhana Soudha|

In front of a noun, `idu` and `adu` shrink to `ee` and `aa`, and the near and far split holds. `idu charger` is a whole sentence, *this is a charger*, while `ee charger` is only *this charger* and still needs a sentence around it.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|this house|ee mane|
|that wall|aa gODe|
|this charger|ee charger|
|this is a charger.|idu charger|

### Notes on the Locative

The singular locative — *with me* — is used to talk about a quality held within someone.

|English|Kannada|

## Lesson 11 — Family and Kinship

Kannada names a relative more exactly than English does. A sibling term carries the age, and an uncle or aunt term carries which parent's side and whether they are older or younger than that parent. There is no one word for brother, sister, uncle or aunt.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|family|kuTumba|
|mother|amma|
|father|appa|
|elder brother|aNNa|
|younger brother|tamma|
|elder sister|akka|
|younger sister|tangi|
|grandmother|ajji|
|grandfather|taata / ajja|

Both sets of grandparents take the same two words. Kannada does not split maternal from paternal there.

`doDDa` is big and `chikka` is small, and both work as a prefix. `doDD-amma` is big mother and `chikk-amma` is small mother, which marks the aunt as older or younger than your own mother.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|mother's elder sister|doDD-amma|
|mother's younger sister|chikk-amma|
|mother's brother|maava|
|father's elder brother|doDD-appa|
|father's younger brother|chikk-appa|
|father's sister|atte|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|wife|henDati|
|husband|ganDa|
|mother-in-law|atte|
|father-in-law|maava|

`atte` and `maava` do double duty. They name the father's sister and the mother's brother from a child's view, and the mother-in-law and father-in-law from a married person's view. Both husband and wife use them for their own in-laws.

|English|Kannada|

## Lesson 14 — Commands and Requests

The root verb by itself is the command. One suffix on the root raises or lowers the register.

|Level|Form|Example|English|
|---|---|---|---|
|informal singular|the root verb|maaDu|do|

|slang|root plus `-O`|maaDO|do|

The slang form is for very close relations only. It is rude anywhere else. Default to the `-i` form with everybody; nobody has ever been offended by too much respect. Put `dayaviTTu`, please, in front to soften it further.

|Root ends in|Formal ending|Example|

|u|change the `u` to `i`|maaDu → maaDi|

## Lesson 16 — The maaDi Trick

Say an English verb and put `maaDi` after it. Bangalore Kannada accepts this for any verb you do not know, and native speakers use it as much as learners do.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|exercise|exercise maaDi|
|drive|drive maaDi|
|enjoy|enjoy maaDi|
|bill it|bill maaDi|
|e-mail it|e-mail maaDi|

The same trick runs through every tense. Hang the English word on `maaDu` and conjugate `maaDu`.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I am exercising.|naanu exercise maaD-taa iddEne|

|I booked the ticket.|naanu ticket book maaDde|
|charge the phone.|phone charge maaDi|

There is a second half to the trick. For something that happens to you rather than something you do, use `aagu`, to become, in place of `maaDu`.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|

|it got cancelled.|cancel aaytu|

## Lesson 22 — Habitual and Future

Kannada uses one ending for the habit and for the future. Context decides which is meant, and a time word usually does the deciding. `naanu cricket aaDtEne` is both *I play cricket* and *I will play cricket*.

Add `-tEne` to the root verb. There is no separate tense marker as in the present continuous, so the ending does the whole job. Its written form is `-uttEne`, so `maaDuttEne` and `maaDtEne` are the same word.

|English|Root|Habitual and future|
|---|---|---|
|do|maaDu|maaDtEne|
|play a sport|aaDu|aaDtEne|
|drink|kuDi|kuDitEne|
|eat|tinnu|tintEne|
|go|hOgu|hOgtEne|
|read, study|Odu|OdtEne|
|come|baa|bartEne|
|see, look|nODu|nODtEne|
|say, tell|hELu|hELtEne|
|ask, hear|kELu|kELtEne|
|give|koDu|koDtEne|
|learn|kali|kalitEne|
|be, exist, stay|iru|irtEne|
|work|kelsa maaDu|kelsa maaDtEne|
|sleep|nidde maaDu|nidde maaDtEne|

`baa` changes shape before the ending and becomes `bar-`, so it is `bartEne` and never `baatEne`.

This is where the habit parts company from the continuous. The plain ending is a habit or the future; the `-taa iru` of Lesson 18 is right now.

## Lesson 30 — Yes and No Questions

There are two ways to ask a question. This is the easy one: take the plain sentence, change nothing in it, and put `-aa` on the very end. The word order stays, and the tense stays.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|you are Indian|neevu Indian|
|are you Indian?|neevu Indian-aa?|
|you are fine|neevu chennaag-iddIra|
|are you fine?|neevu chennaag-iddIraa?|
|he is a doctor|avanu doctor|
|is he a doctor?|avanu doctor-aa?|
|Raj speaks Kannada|Raj Kannada maataaDtaane|
|does Raj speak Kannada?|Raj Kannada maataaDtaanaa?|
|they did the work|avaru kelsa maaDdru|
|did they do the work?|avaru kelsa maaDdraa?|

The `-aa` replaces the last vowel of the verb, so `iddIra` becomes `iddIraa`, `taane` becomes `taanaa` and `maaDdru` becomes `maaDdraa`. Watch the third person past from Lesson 27, where the statement already ends in that vowel: `maaDda` is he did and `maaDdaa` is did he do it, and only the length separates them. After an English noun the noun stays whole and the ending hangs off the end — `Indian-aa`, `doctor-aa`.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|are you coming?|neevu bartIraa?|
|is lunch done?|ooTa aaytaa?|
|do you know Kannada?|nimage Kannada gottaa?|
|is this right?|idu sariyaa?|
|is he at home?|avanu mane-alli iddaanaa?|
|do you want water?|nimage neeru bEkaa?|
|did you see this?|idanna nODdraa?|

The ending glues onto the last vowel: `sari` becomes `sariyaa`, `ide` becomes `ideyaa`, `bEku` becomes `bEkaa`.

# Intermediate

Lesson 34 to Lesson 69. The Beginner block is enough to greet people, buy vegetables and say what you did yesterday. This block is the grammar that turns words into sentences, then the vocabulary and the situations you actually stand in.

## Lesson 34 — Negation, the Three Noes

Kannada has three separate noes, and picking the wrong one is the mistake that marks a beginner. You have met all three inside the tenses; here is when each one applies.

|Kannada|Use|Example|
|---|---|---|
|illa|there is not, do not, did not|nanage gottilla — I do not know|
|alla|it is not, when naming a thing|idu pen alla — this is not a pen|
|bEDa|do not want, do not do it|nanage neeru bEDa — I do not want water|

`illa` denies an action or an existence. `alla` denies an identity. `bEDa` refuses.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I do not work.|naanu kelsa maaDolla|
|I did not work.|naanu kelsa maaDlilla|
|I am not working.|naanu kelsa maaD-taa illa|
|I am not at home.|naanu mane-alli illa|
|he is not coming.|avanu barolla|
|he did not come.|avanu barlilla|
|this is not a pen.|idu pen alla|
|it is not good.|chennaag-illa|
|do not go.|hOg-bEDa|
|please do not go.|hOg-bEDi|

The three tense negatives sound alike, so drill them as a set: `maaDolla` I do not, `maaDlilla` I did not, `maaD-taa illa` I am not doing.

`illa` also joins other words to make their negatives. `ishTa illa` is dislike, `gottilla` is do not know, `saadhya illa` is not possible, `hushaaru illa` is not well.

## Lesson 35 — Noun Cases

English puts *to*, *from*, *with*, *by* and *in* in front of the noun. Kannada puts the same words after the noun, hung on the end of it. That is the whole difference, and the endings that do it are the cases. Lesson 9 already did this for pronouns.

Strip a sentence to bare words, then add the endings, and nothing moves.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|Raj went school bus Pooja|Raj school bassu Pooja hOda|
|Raj went to school by bus, with Pooja|Raj school-ge bass-alli Pooja jote hOda|

`mane` means house. Every ordinary noun takes these six shapes.

|Case|Kannada|English|
|---|---|---|
|nominative|mane|the house|
|accusative|maneyannu, maneyanna|the house, as object|
|dative|manege|to the house|
|genitive|maneya|of the house|
|ablative|maneyinda|from the house|
|locative|maneyalli|in the house|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I am going home.|naanu mane-ge hOg-taa iddEne|
|I came from the house.|naanu mane-inda bande|
|the door of the house|maneya baagilu|
|who is in the house?|mane-alli yaaru iddaare?|

**The dative, `-ge`.** This is the ending you will reach for most, and it comes in three shapes. After `-e` or `-i` it is `-ge`, after `-u` it is `-ige`, and after `-a` it is `-kke`. An English word usually just takes `-ge`: `office-ge`.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|to the house|mane-ge|
|to Bangalore|Bengalur-ige|
|to the book|pustaka-kke|
|to me|nanage|
|to the temple|dEvasthaana-kke|
|to it, therefore|adakke|
|I am going to the office.|naanu office-ge hOg-taa iddEne|
|Raj gave a gift to Geeta.|Raj Geeta-ge gifTu koTTa|
|the doctor has come home.|doctor-avaru mane-ge bandru|

`-avaru` after a name or a job word is the polite marker, and it pulls the plural verb ending.

The same `-kke` hangs on a verb and turns it into a purpose.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|Raj is coming to study.|Raj Oda-kke bar-taa iddaane|
|Geeta is going to the canteen to eat.|Geeta tinna-kke canteen-ge hOg-taa iddaaLe|

The canteen sentence reverses the English order — it reads *to eat, to the canteen, going*.

**The object marker, `-annu`.** In speech it is `-anna`. It says which noun receives the action, the job English does with word order alone.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|Raj hit the ball.|Raj ball-anna hoDida|
|the ball hit Raj.|Raj-anna ball hoDitu|
|Raj bit the dog.|Raj naayi-anna kacchida|
|the dog bit Raj.|Raj-anna naayi kacchitu|

Both sentences of each pair open with `Raj`, so the order carries nothing. The ending decides, and the verb agrees with the doer.

**The ablative, `-inda`.** From.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I am coming from India.|naanu India-dinda bar-taa iddEne|
|Julia has come from Canada.|Julia-avaru Canada-dinda bandru|
|I am going from Chennai to Delhi.|naanu Chennai-inda Delhi-ge hOg-taa iddEne|

The last sentence carries two endings at once and keeps them in the English order.

**The locative, `-alli`.** In and at, and it is the same `alli` that means there.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I live in New York.|naanu New York-alli irtEne|
|he is in the bus.|avanu bass-alli iddaane|
|my college is in Hebbal.|nanna kaalEju Hebbal-alli ide|
|I work at Google.|naanu Google-alli kelsa maaDtEne|

A noun ending in `a` takes a `d` before `-alli`, `-inda` and `-a` — `baNNa-dalli` in the colour, `kuTumba-dalli` in the family, `pustaka-dinda` from the book.

## Lesson 36 — Postpositions

Three more words follow the noun the way the cases do, but stay separate words.

|English|Kannada|Follows|
|---|---|---|
|with, by|jote|the possessive — `nanna jote`|
|for|gOskara|the dative — `nana-gOskara`|
|near, and have|hattira|the possessive — `nanna hattira`|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|Geeta goes to school with Pooja.|Geeta Pooja jote school-ge hOgtaaLe|
|John spoke with his friend.|John avana friend-jote maataaDda|
|eat the chutney with the dosa.|chutney-anna dOse-jote tinni|
|Elton sang a song for Diana.|Elton Diana-gOskara haaDu haaDda|
|Raj is waiting for me.|Raj nana-gOskara kaay-taa iddaane|
|Geeta went to Delhi for her internship.|Geeta avaLa internship-gOskara Delhi-ge hOdaLu|

Kannada also has the benefactive `-aagi` for *for*, built straight on the dative — `nanagaagi` for me, `avanigaagi` for him. Both are correct and `gOskara` is the more common in speech.

**Kannada has no verb to have.** `hattira` does the job, and the sentence reads *the thing is near the person*.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|Raj has my book.|Raj-hattira nanna pustaka ide|
|I have your book.|nanna hattira nimma pustaka ide|
|I do not have change.|nanna hattira chillare illa|
|near the school there is a church.|school-hattira ondu church ide|
|Coorg is near Mysuru.|Coorg Mysuru hattira barutte|

`Raj-hattira nanna pustaka ide` reads *my book is near Raj*, and that is how you say Raj has my book.

Nine more words behave the same way and follow the noun.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|above|mEle|
|below|keLage|
|ahead|munde|
|behind|hinde|
|inside|oLage|
|outside|horage|
|beside|pakkadalli|
|around|suttaloo|
|until|varege, tanaka|

|Pronoun|to — `-ge`|object — `-anna`|from — `-inda`|in — `-alli`|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|naanu|nanage|nannanna|nanninda|nannalli|
|naavu|namage|nammanna|namminda|nammalli|
|neenu|ninage|ninnanna|ninninda|ninnalli|
|neevu|nimage|nimmanna|nimminda|nimmalli|
|avanu|avanige|avananna|avaninda|avanalli|
|avaLu|avaLige|avaLanna|avaLinda|avaLalli|
|avaru|avarige|avaranna|avarinda|avaralli|
|adu|adakke|adanna|adarinda|adaralli|

|Pronoun|with — jote|for — gOskara|have — hattira|

English changes `I` to `me` and stops there. Kannada changes every pronoun for every ending, so `naanu` becomes `nanage` to me, `nannanna` me, `nanninda` from me and `nannalli` in me. Two shapes to watch: `jote` and `hattira` follow the possessive, while `gOskara` follows the dative, so `avanige` gives `avani-gOskara` and `adakke` gives `adakkOskara`.

## Lesson 38 — Wants, Likes and Knowledge

These all put the person in the dative — `nanage`, `ninage`, `avanige` — and never in the subject. Kannada says *to me this is wanted* where English says *I want this*. It feels backwards and it is the single most useful pattern in the language.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I want water.|nanage neeru bEku|
|I do not want water.|nanage neeru bEDa|
|I want a glass of water.|nanage ondu glassu neeru bEku|
|I want a ticket.|nanage ticket bEku|
|I like this.|nanage idu ishTa|
|I like cricket very much.|nanage cricket tumbaa ishTa|
|I like him.|nanage avanu ishTa|
|I do not like this.|nanage idu ishTa illa|
|I like playing cricket.|nanage cricket aaD-Odu ishTa|
|I like learning Kannada very much.|nanage Kannada kaliy-Odu tumbaa ishTa|
|I know Kannada.|nanage Kannada gottu|
|I do not know Kannada.|nanage Kannada gottilla|
|I can speak Kannada.|nanage Kannada barutte|
|I understood.|nanage artha aaytu|
|I did not understand.|nanage artha aagilla|
|I am hungry.|nanage hasivu aagtide|
|I am thirsty.|nanage baayaarike aagtide|

Three words carry the pattern and each has its own negative. `bEku` is want and its negative is `bEDa`. `ishTa` is like and its negative is `ishTa illa`. `gottu` is know and its negative is `gottilla`.

The name of the first course is this pattern with `nanage` dropped, which is how people really say it.

`-Odu` on a verb makes it a noun, and Lesson 44 has the rest of that ending.

## Lesson 39 — Can, May and Must

English puts a modal verb in front of the main verb. Kannada puts a suffix on the end of the root verb instead, and nothing moves.

|English|Suffix|
|---|---|
|may, might|-bahudu|
|must, should|-bEku|
|have to, no way out|-lE bEku|

The root drops its final `-u` before the suffix. A root ending in `-i` keeps it, and for have to it takes `-yalE bEku`.

|English|Root|may|must|have to|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|do|maaDu|maaD-bahudu|maaD-bEku|maaD-lE bEku|
|study|Odu|Od-bahudu|Od-bEku|Od-lE bEku|
|run|ODu|OD-bahudu|OD-bEku|OD-lE bEku|
|learn|kali|kali-bahudu|kali-bEku|kaliyalE bEku|
|come|baa|bar-bahudu|bar-bEku|bar-lE bEku|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I must do it.|naanu maaD-bEku|
|you must come.|neevu bar-bEku|
|you may do it.|neevu maaD-bahudu|
|may I do it?|naanu maaD-bahudaa?|
|I might run a marathon.|naanu marathon OD-bahudu|
|I should run a marathon.|naanu marathon OD-bEku|
|I have to run a marathon.|naanu marathon OD-lE bEku|
|you may speak Kannada here.|neevu illi Kannada maataaD-bahudu|
|I should exercise every day.|naanu prati dina exercise maaD-bEku|
|I have to learn to cook.|naanu aDige maaD-Odu kaliyalE bEku|
|it is possible.|saadhya ide|
|it is not possible.|saadhya illa|
|it will not work out.|aagolla|
|it is done.|aaytu|

`bEku` is want in Lesson 38 and must here. The difference is only whether a verb sits in front of it: `neeru bEku` is I want water, `maaD-bEku` is I must do.

`bEku` takes either subject. `naanu hOg-bEku` and `nanage hOg-bEku` both mean I have to go. Use the dative one when the need is put on you rather than chosen, which is how Lesson 60 says I have to go to Jayanagar.

`-bahudu` only ever grants permission or allows a possibility, so it cannot say can in the sense of a skill. For that, put the person in the dative and use the verb come, as Lesson 38 does: `nanage Kannada barutte`, Kannada comes to me. And for can in the sense of able to, Lesson 44 has `-Oke aagutte`: `nanage bar-Oke aagolla`, I cannot come.

## Lesson 40 — Conditionals

Add `-re` to the past stem to get if.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|if you come|neevu bandre|
|if you go|neevu hOdre|
|if you do|neevu maaDidre|
|if it rains|maLe bandre|
|if not, otherwise|illa andre|
|if you come, call me.|neevu bandre, nanage phone maaDi|
|if it rains, I will not come.|maLe bandre, naanu barolla|

The negative if is `-adidre`: `neevu baradidre`, if you do not come, and `maaDadidre`, if you do not do it.

## Lesson 41 — Joining Sentences

A conjunction joins two sentences into one. Kannada puts it between the two halves, exactly where English does, and neither half changes. `anta` is the one exception, and Lesson 42 is about it.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|and|mattu|
|or|athava|
|but|aadre|
|so, therefore|adakke, aaddarinda|
|because|yaake andre, yaakendre|
|again, and then|matte|

`mattu` joins two nouns, and it also joins two subjects — in which case one verb serves both and it turns plural.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I want coffee and tea.|nanage kaapi mattu chaa bEku|
|Ram is walking.|Ram naDi-taa iddaane|
|Shyam is walking.|Shyam naDi-taa iddaane|
|Ram and Shyam are walking.|Ram mattu Shyam naDi-taa iddaare|

`athava` leaves the verb singular, because only one of the two does the thing.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|Ram or Shyam is walking.|Ram athava Shyam naDi-taa iddaane|
|do you want tea or coffee?|nimage tea bEkaa athava coffee bEkaa?|

The joined question repeats `bEkaa` in both halves and drops the second `nimage`.

`aadre` is but, and a comma goes before it.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I will come, but late.|naanu bartEne, aadre tadavaagi|
|Ram is walking, but Shyam is running.|Ram naDi-taa iddaane, aadre Shyam OD-taa iddaane|

`adakke` and `aaddarinda` are therefore. Both put the cause first and the result second. `adakke` is `adu` plus the dative `-kke` from Lesson 35 — literally *to that*.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|it is raining, so I will not come.|maLe bar-taa ide, adakke naanu barolla|
|Geeta is coming home, therefore Raj made coffee.|Geeta mane-ge bar-taa iddaaLe aaddarinda Raj coffee maaDda|

`yaake andre` is because, and it is the mirror of `adakke`. The same two clauses swap places: the result comes first and the cause second, with a comma before the conjunction. In fast speech the two words join into `yaakendre`.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I did not come because I was unwell.|naanu bandilla, yaake andre nanage hushaaru iralilla|
|Raj made coffee, because Geeta is coming home.|Raj coffee maaDda, yaakendre Geeta mane-ge bar-taa iddaaLe|

`hushaaru` was take care in Lesson 3, and its root sense is well and alert, so `hushaaru illa` is not well. And `andre` on its own is *if you say*, so it also means *means*: `gottilla andre Enu?` is what does gottilla mean.

## Lesson 42 — Reported Speech with Anta

`anta` closes a quote. It comes after the words that were said, never before them, so the whole order flips: Kannada gives what was said, then `anta`, then who said it.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|he said he is coming.|avanu bar-taa iddEne anta hELida|
|she said she will not come.|avaLu barolla anta hELidaLu|
|Murthy said he will go jogging every day.|prati dina jogging hOgtEne anta Murthy-avaru hELidru|
|my name is Sourav.|nanna hesaru Sourav anta|
|what did you say?|Enu anta hELdri?|
|they call it dose.|adakke dOse anta hELtaare|

The rule no table can show: **the quoted part keeps the speaker's own person.** *He said he is coming* is `avanu bar-taa iddEne anta hELida`, with *I am coming* sitting inside the quote.

`anta` does four more jobs beyond quoting, and Lesson 78 has them.

## Lesson 43 — Chained Verbs

English joins two actions with *and*. Kannada has no *and* for verbs. The first verb changes shape instead, and only the last verb in the line carries the tense and the person. This one habit is what turns a page of short sentences into normal speech.

|Verb|Chained form|English|
|---|---|---|
|maaDu|maaDi|having done|
|hOgu|hOgi|having gone|
|baa|bandu|having come|
|tinnu|tindu|having eaten|
|nODu|nODi|having seen|
|hELu|hELi|having said|
|koDu|koTTu|having given|
|kuDi|kuDidu|having drunk|
|kELu|kELi|having heard|
|tagO|tagonDu|having taken|
|ELu|eddu|having got up|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I ate and came.|naanu tindu bande|
|I will finish work and come.|naanu kelsa mugisi bartEne|
|take it and go.|tagonDu hOgi|
|come and see.|bandu nODi|
|he said it and left.|avanu hELi hOda|
|I bathed, ate and went to the office.|naanu snaana maaDi, ooTa maaDi, office-ge hOde|

For a root ending in `u`, the chained form is spelled the same as the polite imperative from Lesson 14. `maaDi` is both *please do* and *having done*. Position decides which, and it never confuses a listener.

Do not mix it up with `-taa` from Lesson 18. `maataaD-taa bande` is I came while talking. `maataaDi bande` is I talked, then came.

## Lesson 44 — Verbal Nouns and the to Form

Two endings turn a verb into something a sentence can carry around.

`-Odu` makes it a noun, the doing of the thing.

|Verb|Noun form|English|
|---|---|---|
|maaDu|maaD-Odu|doing|
|hOgu|hOg-Odu|going|
|baa|bar-Odu|coming|
|tinnu|tinn-Odu|eating|
|kali|kaliy-Odu|learning|
|aaDu|aaD-Odu|playing|
|maataaDu|maataaD-Odu|speaking|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I know how to cook.|nanage aDige maaD-Odu gottu|
|I do not know how to swim.|nanage eej-Odu gottilla|
|speaking Kannada is hard.|Kannada maataaD-Odu kashTa|
|I like playing cricket.|nanage cricket aaD-Odu ishTa|
|what to do.|Enu maaD-Odu|

`-Oke` means in order to. Some speakers say `-akke`, which is the dative from Lesson 35 doing the same job, and both are heard.

|Verb|to Form|English|
|---|---|---|
|maaDu|maaD-Oke|to do|
|nODu|nOD-Oke|to see|
|tinnu|tinn-Oke|to eat|
|baa|bar-Oke|to come|
|hELu|hEL-Oke|to say|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I came to see you.|naanu nimmanna nOD-Oke bande|
|is there anything to eat?|tinn-Oke Enaadroo ideyaa?|
|I do not have time to come.|nanage bar-Oke time illa|
|it is hard to say.|hEL-Oke kashTa|

`-Oke` plus `aagutte`, it happens, is how you say can in the sense of able to. Put the person in the dative, and the negative is `aagolla`.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I can come.|nanage bar-Oke aagutte|
|I cannot come.|nanage bar-Oke aagolla|
|can you do it?|nimage maaD-Oke aaguttaa?|
|I cannot say.|nanage hEL-Oke aagolla|

Written Kannada uses `-uvudu` and `-alu` for these two — `maaDuvudu` and `maaDalu`. You will meet them in print and nowhere in Bangalore.

## Lesson 45 — Describing with a Verb

English hangs a clause on a noun with *who*, *which* or *that*. Kannada has no such word. The verb changes shape and moves in front of the noun, so the whole description reads backwards from English.

|Time|Ending|Example|English|
|---|---|---|---|
|past|-ida|naanu nODida sinima|the film I saw|
|present or future|-O|Jayanagara-kke hOgO bus|the bus that goes to Jayanagar|
|going on now|-taa iro|naanu Od-taa iro pustaka|the book I am reading|
|did not happen|-ada|naanu tinnada ooTa|the food I did not eat|

|English|Kannada|

## Lesson 46 — The KoLLu Ending

`koLLu` hangs on a chained verb from Lesson 43 and turns the action back on the doer. Lesson 15 already used it in `tagO` and `kootukO` without saying why. In speech it shrinks to `-kO` or `-ko`, and the whole word contracts around it.

## Lesson 59 — Conversation, the Saree Shop

One whole conversation in a saree shop. It teaches no new rule; everything in it is Lesson 14, Lesson 19, Lesson 30 and Lesson 35 put to work.

|Speaker|English|Kannada|
|---|---|---|
|Shopkeeper|come, please come! buy sarees!|banni banni! seeregaLu khareedisi!|
|Shopper|please show this green coloured saree|ee hasiru baNNada seere tOrisi|
|Shopkeeper|here you go, have a look|tagOLi, nODi|
|Shopper|what is this called?|idara hesaru Enu?|
|Shopkeeper|this is a Kanchipuram silk saree|idu Kanchipuram silk seere|
|Shopper|what is special in a Kanchipuram silk saree?|Kanchipuram silk seere-alli Enu special irutte?|
|Shopkeeper|we use a gold thread in this|idar-alli chinnada daarav-anna upayOgistEve|
|Shopper|it is very beautiful. how much is it?|tumbaa sundarav-aagi ide. idu eshTu?|
|Shopkeeper|ten thousand …|hattu saavira …|
|Shopper|you are quoting very high. please reduce it|tumbaa jaasti hEL-taa iddIra. swalpa kaDime maaDi|
|Shopkeeper|if you buy two, I will give a 25% discount|neevu eraDu khareedisi, naanu 25% discount koDtEne|
|Shopper|ok, do you have this design in red?|sari, nim-hattira kempu baNNa-dalli ee design ideyaa?|
|Shopkeeper|yes, there is madam. here you go|houdu ide madam, tagOLi|
|Shopper|please pack both sarees. how much is it?|eraDoo seere-gaL-anna pack maaDi. eshTu aaytu?|
|Shopkeeper|great choice! it is fifteen thousand rupees|oLLe choice! hadinaidu saavira rupaayi aaytu|
|Shopper|here you go. thank you|tagOLi, dhanyavaadagaLu|
|Shopkeeper|thanks madam|dhanyavaadagaLu madam|

Every order in the conversation is the bare polite imperative of Lesson 14 — `banni`, `khareedisi`, `tOrisi`, `nODi`, `tagOLi`, `maaDi`. Nothing is softened, on either side, and that is normal in a shop. It would be blunt anywhere else, and Lesson 79 has the softer forms.

`tagOLi` is take, please, and it carries *here you go* twice over. The shopkeeper says it as he hands across the saree and the shopper says it as she hands across the money. It is the collapsed form of `tegedukoLLi` from Lesson 46.

Three of the cases from Lesson 35 do the work. `-alli` is in — `seere-alli` in a saree, `idar-alli` in this, `baNNa-dalli` in the colour, where the `d` comes in after the vowel. `-anna` marks the object in `daarav-anna` and `seere-gaL-anna`. `nim-hattira` is the *have* of Lesson 36, so *do you have this design* is word for word *is this design near you*, and it takes the `-aa` question ending of Lesson 30.

`eraDu` is two and `eraDoo`, with the long final `oo`, is both. That ending is Lesson 75.

The price adds up. One saree is `hattu saavira`, so two are twenty thousand, and a quarter off that is the `hadinaidu saavira` the shopkeeper names at the end. `aaytu` is the past of `aagu`, to become, and it does both *it came to* and *how much did it come to*.

Three words end a purchase and this conversation leaves them out: `koDi` give me, `saaku` that is enough, and `nanage bEDa` I do not want it.

## Lesson 61 — Conversation, a Trip to Hampi

One conversation in two scenes — a hotel desk in Hampi, then a taxi at the temple gate. It teaches no new rule; everything in it is Lesson 14, Lesson 30, Lesson 31, Lesson 35 and Lesson 43 put to work. The title of the scene is `Hampi-ge hOgONa`, let us go to Hampi.

|Speaker|English|Kannada|
|---|---|---|
|Receptionist|salutations!|namaskaara!|
|Tourist|salutations!|namaskaara|
|Receptionist|how is your trip going?|nimma trip hEge naDi-taa ide?|
|Tourist|really good! Hampi is very beautiful!|tumbaa chennaagide! Hampi tumbaa sundaravaagide!|
|Receptionist|what is your plan today, sir?|ivattu nimma plaan Enu, sir?|
|Tourist|today we will go to the Vittala temple|ivattu naavu Vittala dEvasthaana-kke hOgtEve|
|Tourist|can you please call a taxi for me?|neevu nana-gOskara taxi kareetIraa?|
|Receptionist|definitely. I will call right away|khanDita. naanu eegalE kareetEne|
|Tourist|thank you|dhanyavaadagaLu!|
|Tourist|which way is the temple from here?|ill-inda dEvasthaana-kke daari elli ide?|
|Taxi driver|go straight from here and take a left|ill-inda nEra hOgi eDagaDe tirugi|
|Tourist|ok. thank you|sari, dhanyavaadagaLu|
|Tourist|wow! the temple is really amazing!|wah! dEvasthaana tumbaa chennaagide!|

Two endings from Lesson 35 carry the whole conversation. `-ge` and `-kke` are to — `Hampi-ge` to Hampi, `dEvasthaana-kke` to the temple. `-inda` is from, so `ill-inda` is from here.

Both kinds of question are here and they never mix. A question that already has a question word takes no ending — `hEge naDi-taa ide?`, `plaan Enu?`, `daari elli ide?`. The one question without a question word takes the `-aa` of Lesson 30, `kareetIraa?`.

`naDi-taa ide` is the present continuous with a thing as its subject, so the verb ends in `ide`. `naDi` is walk, so *how is your trip going* reads word for word as *how is your trip walking*.

`-aagi` turns an adjective into an adverb, and `ide` glued onto it makes a statement. `chennaagi` well becomes `chennaagide` it is good, and `nEra` straight can become `nEra-vaagi`.

The taxi driver's line is the chaining of Lesson 43 rather than a conjunction: Kannada has no *and* for verbs, so `hOgi` changes shape and only `tirugi` closes the line. A course slide will sometimes join the two with `mattu` instead, and a native speaker will not.

|really?|houdaa?|
|isn't it|alva?, taane?|
|I do not know.|gottilla|
|what to do.|Enu maaD-Odu|
|slowly|nidhaanavaagi|
|quickly|bEga|
|also|kooDa|

`maga` is son in Lesson 11 and *dude* between friends, so it goes to people your own age and never to anyone older. `Enu maaD-Odu` is the verbal noun from Lesson 44. It is a shrug, not a real question, and it ends more Kannada conversations than any other phrase.

`hoon` is the yes you will hear most, and `aaytu`, it happened, is the everyday okay — `aaytu, bartEne`, okay, I will come.

# Advanced

Lesson 70 to Lesson 89. The Intermediate block is enough to build any sentence you need. This one is the layer on top, and it exists because a grammatically perfect sentence still marks you as somebody who finished a course rather than somebody who lives here.

Four things separate the two. Helper verbs that carry attitude rather than meaning, and there are five of them. Endings that carry the emphasis English carries with stress. A set of fixed moves for agreeing, refusing, guessing and complaining, which is most of what conversation actually is. And the listening habits that turn a wall of sound into a sentence.

Two warnings. Lessons 85 and 86 are idioms and proverbs. Those vary by family and by district, and a learner who gets one word wrong lands badly, so treat them as recognition material first and speech material second. Lesson 88 names films and channels, which go out of date, so check what is current before you spend a month on any of them.

## Lesson 70 — Compound Verbs with BiDu

`biDu` on its own means leave it or let go. Chained behind another verb, from Lesson 43, it stops meaning that and says the action went all the way through, usually with a shrug or a sting attached. It is the commonest helper in the language and it is in almost every spoken sentence about a finished action.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|naanu maretu biTTe.|I forgot completely.|
|avanu hOgi biTTa.|he already left.|
|naanu hELi biTTe.|I told them, and that is that.|
|naanu tindu biTTe.|I ate the whole thing.|
|nODi biDi.|just have a look.|
|mugisi biDi.|finish it off.|
|bandu biDtEne.|I will come right away.|
|maaDi biDi.|go ahead and do it.|

In speech the helper glues onto the verb and the two become one word: `maretbiTTe`, `hOgbiTTa`, `bandbiDtIni`. That is what the ear gets, so learn the joined form as well as the split one.

`biDu` still works as a plain verb, and it still means leave.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|biDi, parvaagilla.|leave it, no problem.|
|adanna biDi.|drop that.|

## Lesson 71 — Two More Helper Verbs

`haaku` means put or throw. Chained behind a verb it makes the action quick, offhand, or done and dusted.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|maaDi haaki.|just get it done.|
|bardu haaki.|write it down.|
|kaLisi haaki.|send it off.|
|hELi haaki.|go on and tell them.|

`iDu` means keep or place. Chained behind a verb it means do it now so that it is ready later.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|naanu hELi iTTiddEne.|I have already told them, so that is handled.|
|maaDi iDi.|get it done and keep it ready.|
|tandu iTTiddEne.|I brought it and kept it.|

Put the three helpers side by side on one verb and the difference is easy to hear.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|hELi biTTe.|I said it, and it is out now.|
|hELi haakde.|I just went ahead and said it.|
|hELi iTTiddEne.|I have told them in advance.|
|hELkonDe.|I told them about myself.|
|hELi koTTe.|I taught them.|

The last two are `koLLu` and `koDu` from Lesson 46. Five helpers, one verb, five different sentences, and the verb itself never changed.

## Lesson 72 — Motion Verbs as Helpers

`hOgu` and `baa` also ride behind a chained verb. They add direction, and with a state verb they mark a change that already finished.

`hOgu` sends the action away, or says a state tipped over into another one.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|avanu ODi hOda.|he ran off.|
|avanu hELi hOda.|he said it and left.|
|maretu hOytu.|it slipped my mind.|
|aagi hOytu.|it is over and done.|
|haaLaagi hOytu.|it got ruined.|
|haNa kharchaagi hOytu.|the money went.|

`baa` brings the action toward the speaker.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|tagonDu banni.|bring it.|
|karkonDu banni.|bring them along.|
|nODi banni.|go have a look and come back.|
|kELi bande.|I asked and came back.|

`tagonDu banni` and `karkonDu banni` are worth memorising whole. They are how anybody in Bangalore says bring a thing and bring a person, and neither uses a verb that means bring.

## Lesson 73 — Time Clauses

English puts *when*, *before*, *after* and *until* in front of a clause. Kannada hangs an ending on the verb and puts the whole clause first.

|Meaning|Ending|Example|English|
|---|---|---|---|
|when, while|-Ovaaga|naanu barOvaaga|when I come|
|once it has happened|-idaaga|naanu bandaaga|when I came, or once I come|
|before|-Oke munche|naanu hOgOke munche|before I go|
|after|-ida mEle|ooTa maaDida mEle|after eating|
|until|-O tanaka|naanu barO tanaka|until I come|
|the moment that|-ida takshaNa|avanu banda takshaNa|the moment he came|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|call me when you come.|neevu bandaaga nanage phone maaDi.|
|I will finish it before I go.|naanu hOgOke munche mugistEne.|
|come after you eat.|ooTa maaDida mEle banni.|
|wait until I come.|naanu barO tanaka iri.|
|I saw him while I was on my way.|naanu barOvaaga avananna nODde.|
|tell me the moment it is done.|aada takshaNa nanage hELi.|

The two whens split on timing. `-Ovaaga` is while the thing is going on, and `-idaaga` is once it has happened, which works for the future too — `neevu bandaaga` is when you get here.

Keep both apart from Lesson 40. `-dre` is if, and leaves it open. `-aaga` is when, and it assumes the thing happens.

Every ending here but one sits on a describing form from Lesson 45 — `barO`, `maaDida`, `banda`. `munche` is the exception and takes the `-Oke` of Lesson 44 instead, so before going is `hOgOke munche`.

## Lesson 74 — Even If and Even Though

Swap the final `e` of Lesson 40's if for `-oo`, the also of Lesson 75, and if becomes even if: `bandre` if he comes, `bandroo` even if he comes. The written form is `-aroo`, `bandaroo`.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|maLe bandroo|even if it rains|
|avanu bandroo|even if he comes|
|naanu hELidroo|even though I told them|
|eshTu hELidroo|however much I say|
|maLe bandroo, naanu bartEne.|even if it rains, I will come.|
|naanu hELidroo avanu kELlilla.|even though I told him, he did not listen.|

Hold that `oo` long. `bandru`, with a short `u`, is *they came* from Lesson 27, and only the vowel length separates the two — a Lesson 1 pair hiding in the grammar.

The same ending on `aagu`, to become — `aadroo`, even if it is — builds the any words, and these come up constantly.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|Enaadroo|something, anything|
|yaaraadroo|somebody, anybody|
|ellaadroo|somewhere, anywhere|
|yaavaagaadroo|some time, any time|
|hEgaadroo|somehow, anyhow|
|tinnOke Enaadroo ideyaa?|is there anything to eat?|
|yaaraadroo iddIraa?|is anybody there?|
|yaavaagaadroo banni.|come some time.|

## Lesson 75 — Emphasis Endings

English carries emphasis with the voice. Kannada carries it with two endings, and a sentence without them sounds flat even when every word is right.

`-E` means that one exactly, and nothing else.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|naanE|I myself|
|ivattE|today itself|
|illE|right here|
|adE|that same one|
|eegalE|right now|
|naanE maaDtEne.|I will do it myself.|
|ivattE banni.|come today, not tomorrow.|
|illE ide.|it is right here.|
|adE sari.|that is exactly right.|

`-oo` means also, and doubled across two nouns it means both — `naanoo avanoo`, him and me both. Its near neighbour `-O`, with a long o rather than a long u, is a different ending: doubled across two verbs it means whether or not, and on a question word it gives the vague version.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|naanoo bartEne.|I am coming too.|
|avanoo banda.|he came as well.|
|eraDoo|both|
|bartaanO illvO gottilla.|I do not know whether he will come or not.|
|yaarO banda.|somebody came.|
|EnO hELtidda.|he was saying something.|

`yaarO` and `EnO` are the question words from Lesson 31 with `-O` on the end, not `-oo`. The question word plus `-O` gives you the vague version of it: somebody, something, somewhere.

`-oo` is also the ending inside yesterday's even if. `bandre` is if he comes and `bandroo` is even if he comes, and `Enaadroo`, anything, is *even if it is what*.

## Lesson 76 — Echo Words and Doubling

Two habits do more for how native you sound than any amount of vocabulary.

An echo word repeats the noun with its first syllable swapped for `gi` and means *and things like it*. English has nothing like it. Kannada uses it constantly, and a learner who never does is instantly audible.

|Word|Echo|Meaning|
|---|---|---|
|ooTa|ooTa geeTa|food and all that|
|mane|mane gine|the house and so on|
|kelsa|kelsa gilsa|work and whatnot|
|pustaka|pustaka gistaka|books and such|
|coffee|coffee giffee|coffee or something|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|did you eat or anything?|ooTa geeTa aaytaa?|
|is there any work at all?|kelsa gilsa ideyaa?|
|shall we have coffee or something?|coffee giffee kuDiyONaa?|

The rule is one line: keep the length of the first syllable and put `gi` in its place. A long first syllable gives `gee`, a short one gives `gi`.

Doubling a word means more of it, repeatedly, or one at a time.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|bEga bEga|quickly, hurry up|
|swalpa swalpa|little by little|
|nidhaana nidhaana|easy now, slow down|
|bEre bEre|different ones|
|ondondu|one at a time|
|dina dina|every single day|

## Lesson 77 — Sound Words with Anta

Kannada names a sound and then puts `anta` after it, and the pair works as a description. There is no English habit like it and it is everywhere in ordinary speech.

|Kannada|English|
|---|---|
|dhaDaar anta|with a bang|
|sarrr anta|in a rush, in a flash|
|paTa paTa anta|rat-a-tat, one after another|
|thaTT anta|all at once, without warning|
|gaTa gaTa anta|gulping it down|
|jaal anta|with a splash|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|he talks nineteen to the dozen.|avanu paTa paTa anta maataaDtaane.|
|it fell with a bang.|dhaDaar anta bittu.|
|he drank it straight down.|gaTa gaTa anta kuDidu biTTa.|
|it stopped all of a sudden.|thaTT anta nintu hOytu.|

You do not have to produce these. You do have to recognise them, because a speaker who uses one is not saying a word you can look up.

## Lesson 78 — The Rest of Anta

Lesson 42 gave `anta` for quoting. It does four more jobs, and three of them have no quote anywhere in sight.

Naming. `anta` after a name means called or named.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|what is this called?|idanna Enanta hELtaare?|
|they call it dose.|idakke dOse anta hELtaare.|
|a man called Ramesh|Ramesh anta obba|

Reason and purpose. Put the thought in front and `anta` after it.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I came to see you.|nimmanna nODONa anta bande.|
|I bought it because it was cheap.|kammi anta konDkonDe.|
|I did not call because it was late.|tadavaaytu anta phone maaDlilla.|

`andre` is *if you say*, which does duty for *means* and for *because*.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|what does gottilla mean?|gottilla andre Enu?|
|because|yaake andre|
|otherwise, if not|illa andre|

`annOdu` turns the whole thing into a noun, and `antaare` means they say.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|the thing called work never ends.|kelsa annOdu mugiyOdE illa.|
|they say it will rain.|maLe barutte antaare.|
|people say he is a good man.|avanu oLLeyavanu antaare.|

## Lesson 79 — Asking Without Asking

A bare imperative is fine with a shopkeeper and blunt with everybody else. Kannada softens an ask with a small set of fixed moves, and using them is most of what sounding polite means.

Turn the order into a question.

|Direct|Softer|English|
|---|---|---|
|koDi|koDtIraa?|would you give it?|
|maaDi|maaDtIraa?|would you do it?|
|hELi|hELtIraa?|could you tell me?|
|banni|barOke aaguttaa?|would you be able to come?|

Add `swalpa` and an order becomes a favour.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|could I have some water?|swalpa neeru koDtIraa?|
|could you help me out?|swalpa help maaDtIraa?|
|make a little room.|swalpa jaaga koDi.|
|wait just a moment.|swalpa iri.|

Ask permission with `-bahudaa`, the Lesson 39 ending turned into a question.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|may I come?|naanu barbahudaa?|
|may I take this?|idanna tagO-bahudaa?|
|is that all right?|aaguttaa?|

And the part nobody teaches: saying no without the word no.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|let us see.|nODONa.|
|I will let you know later.|aamEle hELtEne.|
|that might be a bit difficult.|swalpa kashTa aagbahudu.|
|I will see.|nODtEne.|

Every one of those means no. When somebody says `nODONa` to you, it means no.

## Lesson 80 — Agreeing and Disagreeing

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|yes|houdu|
|exactly, that is it|adE|
|correct|sari|
|true|nija|
|of course|khanDita|
|that seems right to me.|nanage sari anisutte.|

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|

|it is not like that|haage alla|
|I do not think so.|nanage haage anisalla.|

A flat contradiction is rude here in a way it is not in English. The soft forms do the same work.

|English|Kannada|

## Lesson 81 — Opinions and Guesses

The whole area runs on two words. `anisutte` is it seems, and `irabOdu` is it might be — `ira-bahudu` from Lesson 39, squeezed.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|

## Lesson 83 — Telling a Story in the Past

A story in Kannada is one long chain of verbs from Lesson 43, held together by time words, with a single tensed verb at the end of each stretch. Learn the time words and the chaining does the rest.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|first|modalu|
|then, after that|aamEle|
|at that point|aaga|
|meanwhile|ashTaralli|
|suddenly|thaTTane|
|in the end|konege|
|one day|ondu dina|
|that day|aa dina|

Here is a whole day as one paragraph. Read it out loud.

nenne beLigge naanu bEga eddu, snaana maaDi, tinDi tindu office-ge hOde. daari-alli tumba traffic ittu. aamEle office-alli meeting ittu, adakke naanu lunch maaDlilla. sanje mane-ge banda mEle tumba sustaagittu. konege bEga malkonDe.

Yesterday morning I got up early, bathed, ate breakfast and went to the office. There was a lot of traffic on the way. Then there was a meeting at the office, so I did not have lunch. After I got home in the evening I was worn out. In the end I went to sleep early.

Look at the first sentence. Three chained verbs — `eddu`, `maaDi`, `tindu` — and one tensed verb at the end. English needs four full verbs and an *and* to say the same thing.

## Lesson 84 — Bangalore Code-Mixing

Nobody in Bangalore speaks pure Kannada. Trying to sounds stranger than mixing, and the mixing is not random.

These stay English almost always: office, meeting, bus, auto, phone, time, ticket, doctor, hospital, sorry, thanks, bill, order, plan, party, weekend, school, college, computer, WiFi, charge, block, cross, signal, side, adjust.

These stay Kannada almost always: the family words `amma`, `appa`, `akka`, `aNNa`; the food words `ooTa`, `tinDi`, `haalu`, `neeru`, `mosaru`; and every verb.

That last one is the actual rule, and it is worth more than the two lists. It is the `maaDi` trick of Lesson 16 grown up. Take any English word and hang a Kannada verb on it — `maaDu` for doing it and `aagu` for it happening.

|English|Kannada|
|---|---|
|I will call you.|naanu call maaDtEne.|
|I booked the ticket.|naanu ticket book maaDde.|
|charge the phone.|phone charge maaDi.|
|it is not working.|work aagtilla.|
|send me a message.|nanage message kaLsi.|
|it got cancelled.|cancel aaytu.|
|we got late.|late aaytu.|
|just adjust a little.|swalpa adjust maaDi.|
|where do I get down?|naanu elli iLibEku?|

Numbers go either way. `aivattu rupaayi` and *fifty rupees* are both normal, and most people mix them inside one sentence.

One thing to expect. A shopkeeper will switch to English the moment your Kannada wobbles, because they are being helpful. Answer the next question in Kannada anyway and most of them switch back.

## Lesson 87 — Registers You Will Hear

The same language sounds like several languages depending on who is talking. Knowing which one you are hearing tells you what to expect before you have decoded a word.

|Who|What changes|
|---|---|
|auto and bus|the fastest speech and the heaviest contraction you will meet. `-ri` on everything, most nouns in English. `elli hOgbEku saar?`|
|shop and market|short, number-heavy, few full sentences. `eshTu`, `saaku`, `koDi`. Numbers often in English.|
|office|the most code-mixed of all. Whole English clauses sitting inside a Kannada sentence.|
|older family|the slowest and most complete. Full `-uttEne` endings, more Sanskrit words, the plural for respect every time.|
|news reader|written Kannada read out loud. `maaDuttide`, `iruvudu`, `hELidaru`. Clear, easy, and almost nobody talks this way.|
|film|written Kannada in the serious scenes and street Kannada in the comedy, often faster than real speech in both.|
|North Karnataka|different words and a different tune. `ide` becomes `ait`, how are you is `hengadIri?`, and what are you doing is `En maaDaakattIri?`. You notice it in one sentence.|
|coastal Karnataka|Tulu and Konkani in the mix, quicker vowels, unfamiliar words.|

Train on auto drivers and shopkeepers first, because that is where you will actually open your mouth. News Kannada is the easiest to follow and the least useful to copy.

## Lesson 88 — Where to Listen

Any list of names goes stale. Check what is current before you commit a month to something here.

Films, dialogue-heavy and modern, because period films are written Kannada spoken aloud. Lucia, Ondu Motteya Kathe, Kirik Party, Rama Rama Re, Sapta Sagaradaache Ello. Thithi and Kantara are worth it later, not first, because both are heavy dialect. Aachar and Co, KGF and Gandhada Gudi come up on every recommendation list and are easier to find than they are to learn from.

Television serials speak slowly and repeat themselves, which makes them good listening even when the story is not.

On YouTube, the Danish Sait sketches are fast Bangalore Kannada with the code-mixing from Lesson 84 running at full tilt. Vickypedia, whose Naanu Nandini song went everywhere, and Tharle Box sit in the same register. Kannada news channels sit at the opposite end: slow, clear, and the written form. Kannada interview podcasts sit between the two, and that middle register is the one you actually want.

A method that works better than watching more.

1. Pick one clip of about two minutes.
2. Listen once at full speed. Write down whatever you caught.
3. Listen at 0.75 speed. Write again.
4. Read the subtitles if there are any.
5. Listen once more at full speed.

Repeat the same clip every day for a week. A new clip every day feels like progress and teaches far less.
